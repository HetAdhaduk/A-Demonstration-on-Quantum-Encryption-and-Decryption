Chapter I · Working Model I Classical Asymmetric Security

The RSA Protocol and the Prime Factorization Trapdoor

In 1977, Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman introduced asymmetric public-key cryptography grounded in a mathematical trapdoor one-way function over finite modular rings. Multiplying two large primes p and q to form a public modulus N is computationally effortless, whereas factoring N back into p and q requires sub-exponential time on classical architectures.

Against a 2048-bit modulus, the classical General Number Field Sieve (GNFS) requires approximately 16 million years. Yet under the Store Now, Decrypt Later (SNDL) paradigm, adversaries archive encrypted traffic today to decrypt it once fault-tolerant quantum computers come online.

Prime Modulus, Euler's Totient & Key Reciprocity (Eq. 1.1)
N = p · q ,   φ(N) = (p − 1)(q − 1) ,   e · d ≡ 1 (mod φ(N))
p, q: secret prime factors; e: public encryption exponent; d: private multiplicative inverse modulo φ(N).
Modular Trapdoor Encryption & Decryption (Eq. 1.2)
C ≡ Me (mod N) ,   M ≡ Cd (mod N)
M ∈ ℤN: numerical plaintext residue; C ∈ ℤN: transmitted ciphertext block.
General Number Field Sieve (GNFS) Complexity (Eq. 1.3)
LN[1/3, c] = exp( (c + o(1)) · (ln N)1/3 (ln ln N)2/3 )
Where c = (64/9)1/3 ≈ 1.9229. Sub-exponential scaling stalls classical brute-force attacks at 7% (> 2.4 years in our scaled model).
Chapter II Quantum Information Mechanics

Two-Level Quantum Systems and Coherent Superposition

While a classical transistor bit is restricted to a discrete potential of 0 or 1, a quantum bit—or qubit—is physically instantiated as a two-level quantum state in a complex Hilbert space ℋ2, such as electron spin projection, single-photon polarization, or quantized flux in superconducting transmon circuits.

Prior to measurement, a qubit evolves unitarily in a linear superposition of basis states |0⟩ and |1⟩ with relative phase eiφ. A register of n qubits spans 2n simultaneous amplitudes: 300 qubits exceed the number of atoms in the observable universe. Applying the Hadamard operator (H) maps |0⟩ onto the Bloch equator, where projective measurement collapses the state probabilistically via Born's rule.

Bloch Sphere State Vector Parameterization (Eq. 2.1)
|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ = cosθ2|0⟩ + eiφ sinθ2|1⟩
θ ∈ [0, π]: polar colatitude governing basis weighting; φ ∈ [0, 2π): azimuthal relative quantum phase.
Born Measurement Rule & Hadamard Unitary Gate (Eq. 2.2)
H = 1√2 [1, 1; 1, −1] ,   P(|0⟩) = cos2(θ/2) ,   |α|2 + |β|2 = 1
Complex amplitudes α, β ∈ ℂ yield real measurement probabilities summing to unity.
Exponential Multi-Qubit Register Superposition (Eq. 2.3)
|Ψ⟩ = 2n − 1∑x = 0 cx |x⟩ ,   2n − 1∑x = 0 |cx|2 = 1
An n-qubit quantum register processes 2n orthogonal basis states |x⟩ simultaneously under a single unitary operation.

Chapter III Non-Local Quantum Correlations

Bipartite Entanglement, Bell's Theorem, and Quantum Key Distribution

Quantum entanglement arises when two qubits interact such that their joint tensor-product wavefunction cannot be factored into independent single-particle states. Measuring the spin projection of Alice's particle |ψA⟩ instantaneously fixes the state of Bob's distant partner |ψB⟩.

In Quantum Key Distribution (BB84 and Ekert E91 protocols), security rests upon physical law rather than computational difficulty. By the No-Cloning Theorem, an unknown quantum state cannot be duplicated unitarily. Any interception by an eavesdropper collapses the entangled coherence, violates the CHSH Bell threshold, and introduces an unavoidable Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER ≥ 25%).

Maximally Entangled Bipartite Bell States (Eq. 3.1)
|Φ+⟩ = 1√2 ( |00⟩ + |11⟩ ) ,   |Ψ−⟩ = 1√2 ( |01⟩ − |10⟩ )
|Φ+⟩ yields perfectly correlated spin outcomes; the antisymmetric singlet |Ψ−⟩ yields opposite spins along any axis.
Non-Separability & The Quantum No-Cloning Theorem (Eq. 3.2)
|ΨAB⟩ ≠ |ψA⟩ ⊗ |ψB⟩ ,   ∄ U s.t. U|ψ⟩|0⟩ = eiδ|ψ⟩|ψ⟩
Unitarity forbids cloning arbitrary superpositions; adversary basis mismatches induce QBER ≥ 25%.
CHSH Bell Inequality Quantum Violation (Eq. 3.3)
S = |E(a, b) − E(a, b′) + E(a′, b) + E(a′, b′)| = 2√2 ≈ 2.828 > 2
Classical local realism is strictly bounded by S ≤ 2; quantum entanglement reaches Tsirelson's bound 2√2.
Chapter III-B · Multipartite Correlations GHZ Theorem (1989)

Multipartite Entanglement & Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger States

To coordinate a classified directive among three spatially separated command nodes—designated Alpha (α), Bravo (β), and Charlie (γ)—without transmitting a vulnerable cryptographic key across a classical network, the protocol employs tripartite quantum entanglement, originally formulated by Greenberger, Horne, and Zeilinger (1989) and adapted for Quantum Secret Sharing by Hillery, Bužek, and Berthiaume (1999).

By applying a Hadamard gate to the primary particle followed by cascaded Controlled-NOT (CNOT) interactions across the secondary and tertiary particles, all three qubits become unified into a single non-separable wavefunction |ΨGHZ⟩. Even when distributed across vast distances, projective measurement of any single particle in the computational Z-basis instantaneously fixes the state of the remaining two particles into identical outcomes—exclusively |000⟩ or |111⟩.

Maximally Entangled Tripartite GHZ Wavefunction (Eq. 3.4)
|ΨGHZ⟩ = 1√2 ( |0⟩α ⊗ |0⟩β ⊗ |0⟩γ + |1⟩α ⊗ |1⟩β ⊗ |1⟩γ ) = |000⟩ + |111⟩√2
Cross-term states such as |001⟩, |010⟩, or |101⟩ possess strictly zero probability amplitude (P = 0).
Unitary GHZ State Preparation Circuit (Eq. 3.5)
|ΨGHZ⟩ = CNOTαγ · CNOTαβ · (Hα ⊗ Iβ ⊗ Iγ) |000⟩
All three commanders simultaneously harvest an identical, correlated stream of random bits directly from vacuum quantum fluctuations.

Chapter III-C · Quantum Key Distribution Bennett–Brassard (1984)

The No-Cloning Theorem & Active Eavesdropper Detection

In classical communications, a passive adversary can duplicate electrical or optical signals in transit without altering the original bitstream. Quantum mechanics forbids such covert duplication through the Wootters–Zurek No-Cloning Theorem (1982), which proves that no unitary operator U exists capable of cloning an arbitrary unknown quantum state |ψ⟩.

Within the BB84 Quantum Key Distribution protocol, the transmitter encodes bits across two non-orthogonal, conjugate polarization bases: the rectilinear computational basis Z = {|0⟩, |1⟩} and the diagonal Hadamard basis X = {|+⟩, |−⟩}. If a compromised participant (Commander Charlie) intercepts photons along Channel B, he must guess the encoding basis at random, selecting the wrong basis with probability 1/2 and injecting a minimum 25.0% Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER)—far exceeding the rigorous 11.0% Shor–Preskill security threshold.

Conjugate Pauli Non-Commutativity & Uncertainty (Eq. 3.6)
[σ̂z, σ̂x] = 2iσ̂y ≠ 0 ,   |±⟩ = |0⟩ ± |1⟩√2
Because rectilinear Z and diagonal X polarization observables do not commute, measuring in an incompatible basis randomizes the state.
Intercept-Resend QBER vs. Shor–Preskill Cutoff (Eq. 3.7)
QBEREve = P(wrong basis) × P(bit flip) = 12 × 12 = 25.0% > 11.0%
When telemetry detects QBER > 11.0% on Channel B (α ↔ γ), the protocol flags the compromised node and aborts key exchange.
Chapter IV · Working Model II Quantum Cryptanalysis

Shor's Factorization Algorithm and the Quantum Fourier Transform

Formulated by Peter Shor in 1994, Shor's algorithm breaks RSA encryption in polynomial time 𝒪((log N)3) by reducing prime factorization of N into a modular period-finding problem for f(x) = gx mod N.

A quantum processor prepares a uniform superposition of exponents |x⟩ and entangles them with their modular residues |x, gx mod N⟩. Applying the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) cancels non-periodic amplitudes via destructive interference and amplifies resonant peaks at multiples of frequency s/r. Continued fractions extract the period r, and Euclidean greatest common divisor (gcd) arithmetic isolates primes p and q almost instantaneously.

Modular Order & Difference-of-Squares Identity (Eq. 4.1)
gr ≡ 1 (mod N)  ⟹  (gr/2 − 1)(gr/2 + 1) ≡ 0 (mod N)
For even period r where gr/2 ≢ −1 (mod N), the terms (gr/2 ± 1) share non-trivial prime factors with N.
Unitary Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) (Eq. 4.2)
QFT|x⟩ = 1√Q Q − 1∑k = 0 e2πixk / Q |k⟩
Transforms periodic state spacing Δx = r into constructive interference peaks at wavenumber ratios k/Q ≈ s/r.
Euclidean GCD Prime Extraction (N = 77, g = 8, r = 10) (Eq. 4.3)
gcd(85 − 1, 77) = 7 ,   gcd(85 + 1, 77) = 11
Classical Euclidean greatest common divisor extracts both secret prime factors p and q in polynomial time.

Chapter V · Working Model III NIST Post-Quantum Standardization

Euclidean Lattice Cryptography, CVP, and Learning With Errors

To secure global communications against Shor's algorithm, NIST has standardized Post-Quantum Cryptography (ML-KEM / CRYSTALS-Kyber and ML-DSA / CRYSTALS-Dilithium) grounded in the geometric hardness of the Closest Vector Problem (CVP), Shortest Vector Problem (SVP), and Oded Regev's Learning With Errors (LWE) over Euclidean lattices.

A receiver constructs a secret Good Basis of short, nearly orthogonal vectors (R1, R2) and publishes a unimodularly equivalent Bad Basis of long, nearly parallel vectors (B1, B2) spanning the identical lattice ℒ. Encrypting a message places it at vertex v* plus a Gaussian error perturbation e to yield noisy point C. While the orthogonal basis resolves v* = −2R1 + 2R2 directly, the skewed basis forces an erratic zig-zag path (8B1 − 6B2) that scales exponentially as 2𝒪(n) in high dimensions.

n-Dimensional Lattice & Unimodular Basis Equivalence (Eq. 5.1)
ℒ(B) = { ∑ zibi | zi ∈ ℤ } ,   v* = −2R1 + 2R2 = 8B1 − 6B2
Where B1 = 2R1 + R2 and B2 = 3R1 + R2 (det U = −1) generate the exact same lattice.
Learning With Errors (LWE) Trapdoor & CVP Optimization (Eq. 5.2)
b = (As + e) mod q ,   vclosest = arg minv ∈ ℒ ‖C − v‖2
A ∈ ℤ975×5: public matrix; s: secret vector; e: small error vector masking the algebraic structure.
LWE Ciphertext & Noise-Resilient Threshold Decryption (Eq. 5.3)
Δ = (v − sTu) mod q = (eTr + m · ⌊q/2⌋) mod q
Since |eTr| is small relative to ⌊97/4⌋ = 24, evaluating |Δ − 48| < 24 recovers message bit m ∈ {0, 1} cleanly.
Chapter V-B · Cryptographic Reconstruction Shannon Perfect Secrecy (1949)

(3,3)-Threshold Quantum Secret Sharing & One-Time Pad Decryption

To enforce strict multiparty authorization over the 64-bit classified directive “THUNDERB”, the master quantum key K—distilled from verified GHZ entanglement—is partitioned across Galois Field GF(2) into three cryptographic shares (Kα, Kβ, Kγ) via modulo-2 addition (⊕, exclusive-OR).

Shares Kα and Kβ are generated as independent uniform random bitstreams, while Kγ is defined as Kγ = K ⊕ Kα ⊕ Kβ. The plaintext command M is encrypted using Claude Shannon’s One-Time Pad (OTP): C = M ⊕ K. Any single commander holding Kα, or even two colluding commanders combining Kα ⊕ Kβ, lacks the final entropy mask Kγ; only when all three honest shares unite is the directive unlocked.

Galois Field GF(2) Key Splitting & One-Time Pad Cipher (Eq. 5.4)
K = Kα ⊕ Kβ ⊕ Kγ ,   C = M ⊕ K
Modulo-2 addition (⊕) over 64-bit registers splits the master GHZ key across three distributed command authorities.
Threshold Reconstruction & Shannon Information-Theoretic Secrecy (Eq. 5.5)
M = C ⊕ (Kα ⊕ Kβ ⊕ Kγ)  ⟺  I(M ; C, Kα, Kβ) = 0
Any coalition of fewer than 3 shares yields zero mutual information with the plaintext directive M.

In this project, quantum mechanics is not merely a theoretical backdrop—it directly governs both sides of the modern cryptographic conflict. Specifically, quantum mechanics applies in three distinct stages of our architecture:

STAGE 01 · QUANTUM KEY GENERATION & TRANSMISSION

Physical-Layer Quantum Cryptography (BB84, E91 & GHZ QSS)

STAGE 03 · POST-QUANTUM GEOMETRIC IMMUNITY

Defending Against Quantum Algorithms (Working Model III · LWE)

Concept 01 · Hilbert Space ℋ₂ Chapters II & IV

Quantum Superposition

Unlike a classical bit restricted to a definite 0 or 1, a qubit exists in a coherent linear combination of orthogonal basis states |0⟩ and |1⟩ simultaneously. Applying the Hadamard gate (H) places an n-qubit register into 2n basis states at once—allowing Shor’s algorithm to compute gx mod N for all exponents x in a single unitary step.

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ ,   H|0⟩ = |0⟩ + |1⟩√2
Concept 02 · Projective Measurement Chapters II, IV & V-B

Born’s Rule & Wavefunction Collapse

Observing a superposed quantum state forces it to collapse instantaneously into a single basis eigenstate (|0⟩ or |1⟩) with probabilities governed by the squared modulus of its amplitudes (|α|2 and |β|2). In our project, this intrinsic physical randomness generates unguessable One-Time Pad keys and isolates periodic cosets in Shor’s algorithm.

P(|0⟩) = |α|2 = cos2θ2 ,   |α|2 + |β|2 = 1
Concept 03 · Non-Local Correlations Chapters III & III-B

Quantum Entanglement (Bell & GHZ)

When qubits interact via CNOT gates, they form a non-separable joint state (|Φ+⟩ or tripartite |ΨGHZ⟩) whose outcomes remain correlated regardless of distance. Measuring one qubit immediately fixes the others (|000⟩ or |111⟩), enabling Alice, Bob, and Charlie to establish shared cryptographic keys without sending them across a wire.

|ΨGHZ⟩ = |000⟩ + |111⟩√2 ≠ |ψα⟩⊗|ψβ⟩⊗|ψγ⟩
Concept 04 · Unitary Linearity Chapters III & III-C

The No-Cloning Theorem

Because quantum evolution is strictly linear and unitary, Wootters and Zurek proved that no physical operation U can copy an arbitrary unknown quantum state |ψ⟩. In our BB84 and E91 channels, this prevents an eavesdropper from duplicating photons in transit to measure them later without destroying the original state.

∄ U s.t. U|ψ⟩|0⟩ = eiδ|ψ⟩|ψ⟩
Concept 05 · Non-Commuting Pauli Operators Chapter III-C (BB84)

Heisenberg Uncertainty & Conjugate Bases

In BB84, bits are encoded in non-commuting rectilinear (Z) and diagonal (X) polarization bases ([σ̂z, σ̂x] ≠ 0). By Heisenberg’s Uncertainty Principle, measuring a photon in the wrong basis randomizes its polarization, causing any traitor interception on Channel B to inject a 25.0% Quantum Bit Error Rate (exceeding the 11.0% abort limit).

[σ̂z, σ̂x] = 2iσ̂y ≠ 0 ⟹ QBEREve = 25.0%
Concept 06 · Phase Coherence Chapter IV (Shor's QFT)

Quantum Interference & Fourier Transform

Quantum probability amplitudes carry complex phase factors (e2πixk/Q) that interfere like waves. In Working Model II, the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) uses destructive interference to cancel non-periodic guesses and constructive interference to amplify resonant peaks at multiples of 1/r—cracking RSA’s prime factors in polynomial time.

QFT|x⟩ = 1√Q ∑ e2πixk/Q|k⟩ ⟹ kQ ≈ sr
